         Transport and transport systems during
                         the Roman Empire

At the height of the Roman Empire in the 2nd century AD, it was not uncommon for Romans to
travel long distances by road across Europe and beyond. In fact, it was actually quite easy to
travel, thanks to the empire's highly developed road and shipping networks. It has been
calculated that Roman roads covered a total distance of over 400,000 kilometres, with more
than 120,000 kilometres of this being of the type known as 'public roads' and a second system
classified as 'military roads', both constructed by soldiers. The roads spread across the empire
from Britain in the north to Morocco in the south, from Portugal in the west to Iraq in the east.
Roads played a crucial role in the maintenance of the empire, allowing people and goods to
move relatively swiftly. Tolls were charged for the use of many major roads, with stations where
these tolls were paid often situated at bridges or at city gates.

Having the elaborate road network also meant that Roman troops could be sent rapidly to
various parts of the empire in the event of emergencies, such as internal revolts or external
threats. Therefore, the existence of these roads reduced the number of large and costly
garrisons* needed throughout the empire.

Ordinary Romans would travel in a raeda, a carriage with four wooden wheels covered with iron
and pulled by up to four horses. The raeda was the equivalent of the bus today. It had wooden
benches inside for passengers, and a cloth top or sometimes no top at all. Rich Romans
travelled in a carpentum, which was essentially a luxury vehicle for the wealthy. The carpentum
had four wheels, a wooden arched rooftop and seats. It was pulled by many horses and was
built incorporating a form of suspension to make the ride less bumpy and disagreeable.
Romans also had a vehicle, called a plaustrum, that did not normally carry passengers-what
would be considered a truck today. The plaustrum, constructed with a flat wooden base over
four thick wheels and drawn by two oxen, was designed to carry heavy loads. It was very slow
and could cover only 15 to 25 kilometres per day.

The fastest way to travel over heavily trafficked routes was by the cursus publicus. This was like
a state-run postal service, but was also used to transport government or military officials. A
certificate issued by the emperor was required in order to use the service. A series of stations
with fresh horses were built at short regular intervals of approximately 12 kilometres along the
major road systems. It has been estimated that a typical trip by relays of horses was made at a
rate of 66 to 103 kilometres per day.

Because of their iron-shod wheels, Roman vehicles made a lot of noise and were therefore
forbidden by law from entering the vicinity of big Roman cities during the day. Fortunately, many
roads had stations, called mansiones, where Roman travellers could stop and break their
journey, the equivalent of motorway rest areas today. These sometimes included inns where
tired travellers could drink, eat and sleep. The stations were built by the government at regular
intervals of 25 to 30 kilometres. One of the factors that meant such roads could be safely used

for trade was the fact that they were patrolled by the Roman army. Merchants knew that they
would usually be protected from bandits and highwaymen.

It was not uncommon for well-to-do Romans to travel as tourists to places relatively far away
such as Greece and Egypt. For this, they would often have to sail across the Mediterranean
Sea, but as there were no regular passenger services, they travelled on ships which were
transporting goods. Tourists had to find a ship, get the captain's approval and negotiate a price
with him. Fortunately, there were many merchant ships going back and forth regularly along
established routes in the Mediterranean so tourists had little difficulty finding a ship going to
their chosen destination. Most Roman tourists would travel on the open deck of the ship,
sometimes with hundreds of people crowded together. Food was not included in the price of
transport, so passengers had to supply their own. Certain merchant ships provided cabins at
the rear that could accommodate the richer passengers. Meanwhile, the wealthiest Romans
had their own ships and employed a crew to sail them, much like on big private yachts today.

Travelling by ship in Roman times was relatively fast. For example, going from Brindisium in
Italy to Patrae in Greece would take about three days. Commercial navigation was suspended
during particularly bad storms, when the Mediterranean was too rough and dangerous for ships
to sail.
